01 / WHY IT MATTERS

Language models do not naturally separate instructions from data.

A conventional application can reject malformed input. An LLM is designed to interpret what it receives, so a sentence buried in a document can become an instruction instead of inert content.

SECURITY SIGNAL01

Model behaviour, retrieval architecture, permissions, and downstream tools form one connected security boundary.

02 / THREAT LANDSCAPE

Three risks the seminar put under the microscope.

The white paper focuses on the attack paths students and early-career engineers are most likely to encounter when AI applications move from demo to production.

01
>_

Prompt injection

Malicious content is interpreted as a new instruction. The indirect form is especially dangerous when the instruction is hidden inside data the model retrieves on its own.

DEFENCE

Constrain actions, treat model output as untrusted, and require human approval where consequences matter.

02
DNA

Data & model poisoning

Tampered training or fine-tuning data can shape later behaviour or introduce trigger-based failure modes. Public models, datasets, and libraries widen the AI supply-chain boundary.

DEFENCE

Track provenance, trust boundaries, dependencies, and the origin of every component entering the model pipeline.

03
◫

Data extraction & leakage

A model can expose training fragments, secrets, hidden prompts, or another user's records when retrieval and access controls are too permissive.

DEFENCE

Use least privilege, tenant isolation, secret hygiene, output controls, and retrieval-aware authorization.

03 / FROM THE ROOM

Security becomes real the moment an AI system can reach real data.

The discussion moved past model behaviour into retrieval stores, access controls, downstream tools, and the trust assumptions inherited from third-party components.

“The model is trying to be helpful, and helpfulness is the weakness.”
04 / DUAL USE

The same tooling can amplify both sides.

Defenders use machine learning to identify unusual behaviour in traffic and logs. Attackers can use the same capability to scale phishing, reconnaissance, and repetitive intrusion tasks.

DEFENSIVEAnomaly spotting · log analysis
OFFENSIVEPhishing · scale · automation
05 / START HERE

A practical learning playbook.

The speakers' advice was deliberately hands-on: learn the foundations, build something small, and then test where it breaks.

01
FOUNDATION

Master application-security fundamentals

LLM risks sit on top of ordinary web and application security—not instead of it.

02
BUILD

Stand up a small LLM application

Create something you understand end-to-end before trying to secure larger systems.

03
BREAK

Attack your own controlled environment

Try to expose its system prompt or make it follow an instruction hidden in a document it reads.

04
GO DEEP

Choose defensive tooling or offensive testing

Depth in one direction is more useful than skimming both without practical experience.

$ readiness_check

No expensive lab required. Start with a laptop, a controlled environment, and the willingness to break something you built on purpose.

06 / ABOUT THE EVENT

Campus learning connected directly to industry practice.

The seminar was hosted by the OWASP Student Chapter at Bahria University Lahore Campus under the mentorship of Zunnurain Hussain. Hamza Khan of Tkxel delivered the keynote, with Abdullah Mehtab as co-speaker.

The room mixed students, faculty, and industry participants, and the questions pushed the discussion beyond theory toward the realities of production systems.
